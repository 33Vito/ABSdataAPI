## What do BIS Irving Fisher Committee on Central Bank Statistics (IFC) surveys, IMF SDDS/SDDS Plus reports, and OECD statistical papers say about how central banks source and ingest national macro data (GDP, CPI)?

### Takeaway
Primary BIS IFC surveys (2015–2025), IMF SDDS/SDDS Plus standards, and OECD/SIS-CC documentation show that institutional data exchange between National Statistical Offices (NSOs), central banks, and international bodies is heavily bifurcated: outbound international reporting is largely automated around SDMX, whereas inbound data ingestion and internal compilation remain fragmented across bespoke pipelines, SFTP flat files, competing domain standards, and legacy time-series systems. Central banks are actively trying to move toward metadata-driven "single source of truth" architectures, though macroeconomic real-sector domains (GDP, prices/CPI, labour markets) are typically produced by NSOs rather than central banks and show lower internal SDMX workflow penetration than core central bank financial statistics.

### Cited Findings

#### BIS Irving Fisher Committee (IFC) Surveys & Reports
- **Two benchmark global central bank SDMX surveys (2015 vs. 2023):** The BIS IFC surveyed 53 central banks in August–September 2015 ([BIS IFC Report No. 4, March 2016](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-4-central-banks-use-the-sdmx-standard.pdf)) and updated the survey in 2023 across 52 central bank jurisdictions—representing roughly half of IFC members across Europe, Asia & Oceania, Africa, and the Americas ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).
- **Division of statistical production between NSOs and central banks:** The 2023 IFC survey confirms that central banks predominantly produce five core domains (monetary and financial statistics, financial markets and securities, balance of payments/IIP, payment systems, and supervisory/prudential statistics) and produce statistics far less actively in six domains typically compiled by NSOs or ministries—specifically **GDP and non-financial accounts, prices (CPI), labour market statistics, international trade, government finance statistics, and financial accounts** ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).
- **Asymmetry between outbound reporting and inbound collection:** In the 2023 IFC survey, **81%** of responding central banks rated SDMX as "extremely or very relevant" for **data reporting** (largely driven by mandatory/standardized transmissions to the IMF, BIS, and ECB) and **59%** for **data production**, whereas only **38%** rated it extremely or very relevant for **data collection/ingestion** ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).
- **Time-series vs. granular data management (2023 BIS-IFC survey):** A separate 2023 BIS-IFC survey of 19 central banks and statistical agencies (18 IFC members plus the Bank of England) found that most institutions still treat structured macroeconomic time series (like GDP and CPI) as distinct from other data types in key aspects of ingestion, storage, and access, while aiming—but struggling—to build a **"single source of truth"** where data are collected once, stored in one system, and reused across conjunctural analysis, forecasting, research, and data science ([BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf); [Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023 workshop](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf)).
- **Expansion into linked administrative and alternative data:** Following the 12th IFC Biennial Conference (August 2024) and IFC Guidance Note No. 6, BIS economists note that central banks increasingly complement official NSO macro series by linking administrative records, micro-data registries, and alternative private-sector datasets to counter falling survey response rates and achieve higher timeliness, while warning that secondary data must not crowd out reliable official statistics ([BIS IFC Bulletin No. 66, 2024/2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-bulletin-66-statistics-and-beyond-new-data-decision-making-central-banks); [SUERF Policy Brief No. 1276, October 2025](https://www.suerf.org/publications/suerf-policy-notes-and-briefs/data-integration-in-central-banks-opportunities-and-challenges/)).

#### IMF SDDS / SDDS Plus & International Dissemination Frameworks
- **Machine-to-machine dissemination via National Summary Data Pages (NSDPs):** Under the IMF's Special Data Dissemination Standard (SDDS) and SDDS Plus—administered via the Dissemination Standards Bulletin Board (DSBB)—subscribing countries (coordinating NSOs, central banks, and finance ministries) publish key real-sector (GDP, CPI, employment), fiscal, financial, and external indicators on a single country **National Summary Data Page (NSDP)** that supports automated machine-to-machine SDMX data and metadata exchange ([IMF DSBB SDDS Plus Overview](https://dsbb.imf.org/sdds-plus/overview); [BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).
- **G20 Data Gaps Initiative (DGI) catalyst:** The G20 DGI (Phases 1–3; DGI-3 workplan issued March 2023) explicitly drove cross-agency NSO-to-central-bank standardization, including Recommendation 14 of DGI-3 aimed at working toward an international microdata-sharing standard coordinated with SDMX ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).

#### OECD, Eurostat & European Institutional Exchange Mechanisms
- **OECD / SIS-CC `.Stat Suite` architecture:** The OECD leads the Statistical Information System Collaboration Community (SIS-CC), which maintains the open-source, SDMX-native **`.Stat Suite`** (comprising `.Stat Core` data store, `.Stat Data Lifecycle Manager` for automated back-office ingestion/processing, and `.Stat Suite Data Explorer` with standard APIs and downloads) used by NSOs, central banks, and international agencies to manage the macroeconomic data lifecycle ([OECD / SIS-CC `.Stat Suite`](https://siscc.org/stat-suite/); [BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).
- **Eurostat eDAMIS and ECB EXDI vs. national SFTP channels:** Within Europe, NSOs and National Central Banks (NCBs) transmit official macroeconomic datasets to Eurostat via **eDAMIS (electronic Data Administration and Management Information System)**—now on EDAMIS 4—which enforces automated structural and content pre-validation before official transmission ([Eurostat CROS EDAMIS Pre-validation User Guide, March 2026](https://cros.ec.europa.eu/book-page/edamis-pre-validation-user-guide)). A 2025 Central Bank of Malta case study illustrates the institutional reality: outbound transmissions to the ECB (via **EXDI**) and IMF SDDS+ are SDMX-oriented, whereas domestic data sharing with national authorities still runs over **non-SDMX SFTP** ([Central Bank of Malta, SDMX 2025 Global Conference](https://www.sdmx2025.org/plenary/SESSION_4/Session%204_P2%20-%20SDMX%20Oriented%20Dissemination%20System%20Implementation%20Case%20Study%20at%20the%20Central%20Bank%20of%20Malta%20%28I.%20Cagan%29.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Because NSOs own domestic GDP, CPI, and labour force compilation in most jurisdictions, central banks act primarily as *consumers* rather than *producers* of those series—which helps explain why BIS IFC surveys focus heavily on central banks' outbound financial reporting (BoP, monetary stats) while domestic NSO-to-central-bank ingestion often relies on bilateral arrangements (SFTP, bespoke ETL, or commercial aggregators) rather than uniform SDMX ingestion.
- The gap between 81% SDMX relevance for reporting and 38% for collection signals that many central banks treat official data standards as an external compliance interface rather than an internal ingestion backbone.

### Gaps
- Neither BIS IFC Report No. 17 (February 2025) nor BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4 (December 2023) breaks down the exact percentage of central banks ingesting domestic NSO GDP/CPI releases via direct NSO APIs versus SFTP versus commercial aggregators; survey metrics track statistical standards and storage products rather than specific NSO ingestion channels.
- Public OECD/PARIS21 working papers referenced in IFC Report No. 17 (e.g., *Data flow analysis framework*, 2021) focus primarily on NSO internal data flows rather than quantifying central bank ingestion architectures for NSO series.

## What role does SDMX (Statistical Data and Metadata eXchange) play in NSO-to-central-bank data exchange globally? What are its adoption rates and limitations?

### Takeaway
SDMX (ISO 17369) is the dominant global standard for structured macroeconomic data exchange, with **79%** of surveyed central banks using or implementing it as of the 2023 BIS IFC survey (up from **64%** in 2015) and an additional **14%** evaluating or planning adoption. However, its practical role in NSO-to-central-bank ingestion is constrained by version fragmentation (only **6%** had adopted SDMX 3.0 as of the 2023 survey, even as SDMX 3.1 shipped in May 2025), end-of-pipeline "export-only" usage, competing collection standards (XBRL, ISO 20022, DDI), legacy IT adaptation costs, and shortages of internal SDMX expertise.

### Cited Findings

#### Standards Evolution & Governance
- **ISO standard and sponsor coalition:** Launched in 2001 and standardized as **ISO 17369**, SDMX is jointly sponsored (as of January 2025) by eight international organizations: the **BIS, ECB, Eurostat, ILO, IMF, OECD, UN, and World Bank Group** ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf); [SDMX Official Technical Specifications](https://sdmx.org/?page_id=5008)).
- **Technical specification releases up to 2025:** **SDMX 3.0** was released in **September 2021**—adding support for granular/microdata, geospatial data, hierarchical codelists, enhanced REST APIs, SDMX-JSON, and SDMX-CSV while **deprecating legacy SDMX-EDI**—and **SDMX 3.1** was released in **May 2025** ([SDMX Official Technical Specifications](https://sdmx.org/?page_id=5008); [BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).
- **Global Data Structure Definitions (DSDs):** Global DSDs—coordinated by the international Macro-Economic Statistics Ownership Group (MES OG) and published via the **SDMX Global Registry** (`registry.sdmx.org`)—already exist for major NSO and central bank domains including **national accounts (GDP), consumer prices (CPI), balance of payments, government finance, financial accounts, and (newly co-developed by BIS, Eurostat, IMF, and OECD) property price indicators** ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).

#### Verified Central Bank Adoption Rates (2015 vs. 2023/2025)
- **Overall adoption surge:** In the 2023 BIS IFC survey (52 central bank jurisdictions, published February 2025), **79%** of responding central banks were using or implementing SDMX and **14%** were evaluating or planning to implement it (93% combined)—up from **64%** using/implementing and **13%** evaluating/planning (with roughly 25% having no near-term plans) in the 2015 survey of 53 central banks ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf); [BIS IFC Report No. 4, March 2016](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-4-central-banks-use-the-sdmx-standard.pdf)).
- **Domain-level adoption:** Around **60%** of central banks producing monetary/financial statistics, financial market/securities statistics, and balance of payments/IIP use SDMX, and adoption is high among the smaller set of central banks producing government finance and financial accounts. By contrast, SDMX is less adopted in **GDP/non-financial accounts, prices (CPI), labour market statistics, and international trade** (though central banks report plans to expand SDMX beyond reporting in these domains), and lower in **payment systems (53%)** and **supervisory/prudential statistics (44%)** ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).
- **Open-source SDMX tooling ecosystem:** Central banks reported using **72 distinct SDMX tools**: **51%** of installations are open-source (25 unique tools actively used, led by SDMX Converter, SDMXSource, Fusion Metadata Registry [FMR], SDMX Global Registry, `pandaSDMX`, `.Stat Suite`, `rsdmx`, `RJSDMX`, `SDMXthon`, and BIS's `pysdmx`), **36%** are in-house custom tools, and **13%** are commercial. **83%** of respondents were satisfied or very satisfied with open-source SDMX tools ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).

#### Key Limitations & Structural Bottlenecks
- **Severe version lag (SDMX 2.0/2.1 lock-in vs. 3.0/3.1):** In the 2023 survey, **SDMX 2.1** (from 2011) accounted for **59%** of version responses and **SDMX 2.0** still accounted for **35%**, while **SDMX 3.0 had been adopted by only 6%** of responding central banks (though ~two-thirds of older-version users plan to migrate eventually, with one-fourth citing microdata needs). Many central banks explicitly stated they would **delay upgrading until recipient international organizations mandate SDMX 3.0 submissions** ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf); [Central Banking Journal, BIS survey coverage](https://www.centralbanking.com/central-banks/economics/data/7972409/central-banks-struggling-to-adopt-latest-data-standards-bis-survey)).
- **Multi-version fragmentation inside the same institution:** Because reporting requirements from different recipient organizations evolve at different speeds, individual central banks frequently run **multiple SDMX versions concurrently** across departments, eroding cross-domain synergies ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).
- **"Last-mile export" rather than end-to-end ingestion:** Central banks commonly collect and process data using internal proprietary/bespoke schemas and **convert datasets into SDMX only at the final stage before external reporting**—as explicitly documented at the Central Bank of Malta, which maintains its own internal model for collection/compilation and uses a separate conversion step (migrating via R) for SDMX dissemination ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf); [Central Bank of Malta, SDMX 2025 Global Conference](https://www.sdmx2025.org/plenary/SESSION_4/Session%204_P2%20-%20SDMX%20Oriented%20Dissemination%20System%20Implementation%20Case%20Study%20at%20the%20Central%20Bank%20of%20Malta%20%28I.%20Cagan%29.pdf)).
- **Competing data standards across domains:** Central banks must operate parallel technical stacks alongside SDMX: **52%** use or implement **XBRL** (plus 6% planning) for supervisory/prudential bank reporting (25% rate XBRL-to-SDMX conversion a moderate priority), **ISO 20022** for payment systems, **DDI** for record-level microdata/surveys, and **ISO 19115/19111** for geospatial/climate data. Meanwhile, SDMX's companion **Validation and Transformation Language (VTL)** has limited tool availability, with only **16%** using or planning to use it within two years ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).
- **Resource, IT adaptation, cybersecurity, and tooling fragmentation barriers:** The top barriers cited for both initial SDMX adoption and SDMX 3.0 migration are **lack of resources**, **the cost/effort of adapting internal legacy IT tools**, **lack of compelling business justification**, **shortages of specialized internal SDMX modelling knowledge** (particularly in Africa and Asia), **cybersecurity vetting hurdles for open-source tools**, and **fragmentation across dozens of niche SDMX tools** used by only one or two institutions ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).

### Inferences
- While SDMX has succeeded as the lingua franca for *official cross-border dissemination* (NSO/NCB → IMF/BIS/ECB/Eurostat), its low adoption in *collection* (38%) and slow uptake of SDMX 3.0 (6%) mean that NSO-to-central-bank ingestion still frequently requires custom adapters or falls back to non-SDMX channels unless both agencies co-invest in shared DSDs and registries.
- Open-source public-good initiatives—especially BIS Open Tech / `sdmx.io` (FMR, `pysdmx`, `.Stat Suite`, SDMX Test Compatibility Kit)—are explicitly targeting the fragmentation and expertise bottlenecks identified in the 2023 survey.

### Gaps
- The 2023 BIS IFC survey polled **central banks**, not NSOs directly; therefore, comparable 2024–2026 global SDMX adoption percentages specifically for NSOs (especially comparing OECD NSOs vs. emerging-market NSOs for GDP/CPI API publication) are not established by the BIS IFC dataset.
- Because the BIS IFC Report No. 17 survey was conducted in 2023 (published February 2025), actual 2025–2026 adoption figures for SDMX 3.0 and the newly released SDMX 3.1 (May 2025) have not yet been re-measured in a follow-up global central bank survey.

## Why do many central banks still rely heavily on commercial aggregators (especially Haver Analytics, Macrobond, Bloomberg, CEIC) even when NSO APIs are free?

### Takeaway
Central banks—including major institutions like the ECB and national central banks/treasuries visible in public procurement records—continue to procure commercial macroeconomic aggregators (**Haver Analytics, Macrobond, Bloomberg, CEIC, LSEG/Refinitiv**) primarily for cross-country monitoring, forecasting, and economist workflows rather than replacing domestic official bilateral feeds. Even though raw NSO APIs are free, commercial aggregators solve five costly institutional pain points: normalizing heterogeneous foreign NSO schemas, preserving **point-in-time (PIT) revision/vintage histories**, insulating operations from breaking NSO endpoints under SLAs, providing curated break-in-series metadata, and delivering turn-key integration with Excel, MATLAB, EViews, R, Python, and cloud warehouses.

### Cited Findings

#### Verified Institutional Procurement & Official Ecosystem Evidence
- **Public central bank and public-sector procurement of aggregators:** Major monetary and fiscal authorities actively tender or issue sole-source justifications for commercial macroeconomic time-series platforms. Examples in public procurement portals include European Central Bank tenders for the provision of current and historical economic and financial data ([EU Tenders Electronic Daily (TED) Notice 174270-2019](https://ted.europa.eu/en/notice/-/detail/174270-2019)) and U.S. federal procurement notices/sole-source justifications on SAM.gov for proprietary macroeconomic data software packages ([SAM.gov Opportunity Notice](https://sam.gov/opp/fd90de6ad08e4b8e8357d99926724931/view)).
- **Documented vendor positioning and technical capabilities:**
  - **Macrobond** positions its platform as an integrated macroeconomic and financial time-series database combined with charting, formula engines, Excel/Python/R integration, and automated workflow tooling used by central banks and public-sector analysts ([Macrobond Official Documentation](https://help.macrobond.com/application-overview/mb-introduction/)).
  - **Econometric software ecosystem & build-vs-buy trade-offs:** BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4 documents that central bank analytical desks rely on commercial econometric suites such as **MATLAB, STATA, and EViews** alongside proprietary time-series databases, and emphasizes that commercial licensing fees must be weighed against the substantial internal cost of developing and maintaining complex standalone data ingestion and curation architectures—while serving diverse internal personas including **conjunctural analysts, forecasters, researchers, and data scientists** ([BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).
  - **Key commercial retention factors cited by central banks:** In the 2023 BIS-IFC survey, central banks reported that commercial/proprietary time-series systems remain attractive due to stability and vendor support, dedicated functionality for structured macro time series, regulatory/security compliance, and deep embedding in internal analytical workflows that makes replacement costly ([BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).

### Inferences
- Central banks rarely treat commercial aggregators and direct NSO feeds as mutually exclusive: **domestic headline releases** (for statutory monetary policy briefings or embargoed access) are typically ingested directly from the national NSO via official channels (or loaded into an internal institutional time-series repository), while **commercial aggregators (Haver Analytics, Macrobond, Bloomberg, CEIC, LSEG/Refinitiv)** serve as the dominant ingestion and analytical layer for **international/peer-country macro series, market-price series, consensus forecasts, and ad hoc economist research**.
- Synthesizing the BIS IFC findings on multi-version standard fragmentation (SDMX 2.0/2.1/3.0 plus non-SDMX bilateral channels) and internal IT resource constraints, the economic logic for paying commercial aggregators despite free NSO APIs centers on four practical pain points: (1) normalizing heterogeneous foreign NSO APIs, date conventions, and units into one uniform query schema; (2) preserving **point-in-time (PIT) revision/vintage histories** where free NSO APIs typically overwrite revised historical observations; (3) offloading ETL maintenance and endpoint-breakage risk under enterprise SLAs; and (4) providing curated break-in-series metadata and turn-key add-ins for Excel, MATLAB, EViews, R, and Python.

### Gaps
- **No public central bank market-share survey:** Neither the BIS IFC nor the IMF publishes a quantitative survey reporting the exact percentage of central banks subscribing to Haver Analytics vs. Macrobond vs. Bloomberg vs. CEIC vs. LSEG/Refinitiv, nor average annual expenditure per vendor.
- **Limited directly verified primary technical documentation on Haver Analytics, CEIC, and Bloomberg:** While secondary search summaries highlight Haver Analytics' DLX connectors (MATLAB, EViews, R), point-in-time vintage tracking, and Macrobond's Snowflake/Databricks cloud feeds, direct primary technical specifications from Haver, CEIC, and Bloomberg and line-item central bank RFP evaluations (including dynamic web-app details inside EU TED notice `174270-2019` and SAM.gov notices) could not be fully extracted in this pass.

## To what extent do manual Excel/CSV extracts and legacy flat-file processes still persist in central banks (e.g., for ad hoc analysis, non-standard series, or revisions), and what modernization trends (cloud data lakes, Python/R API clients, automated ETL) are underway as of 2025-2026?

### Takeaway
Manual Excel workflows, spreadsheet add-ins (such as the FAME Excel Populator and SDMX Excel add-ins), static Excel web downloads, and non-SDMX flat-file SFTP transfers remain deeply entrenched in central banks—especially for ad hoc economist analysis, legacy briefing packs, and domestic inter-agency exchange. However, as of 2025–2026, central banks are aggressively modernizing along four converging tracks: migrating toward **Python and R programmatic clients** (e.g., `pysdmx`, `pandaSDMX`, `rsdmx`), building **metadata-driven cloud/distributed data lakes and lakehouses** (e.g., BIS's MEDAL on SDMX 3.0, ECB's Apache Hadoop stack), adopting **open-source SDMX registries and portals** (FMR, `.Stat Suite`), and piloting **generative AI / NLP natural-language data discovery** (e.g., IMF StatGPT, OECD–BIS SDMX+AI).

### Cited Findings

#### Persistence of Excel, Static Spreadsheets, and Legacy Flat Files
- **Deep embedding of Excel add-ins in legacy time-series workflows:** In the 2023 BIS-IFC survey of 19 central banks and statistical agencies, one of the primary reasons institutions cited for sticking with legacy FAME databases was that **"it works well with Excel, through the FAME populator add-in,"** alongside high switching costs due to deep embedding in existing analytical spreadsheets and briefing routines ([Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf); [BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).
- **Continued reliance on static Excel files and flat-file SFTP in 2025:** The Central Bank of Malta's 2025 SDMX Global Conference case study documents that even within the Eurosystem, public statistical dissemination still relied partially on **static Excel files**, domestic data sharing with national authorities ran over **non-SDMX SFTP**, and SDMX conversion occurred separately from internal compilation ([Central Bank of Malta, SDMX 2025 Global Conference](https://www.sdmx2025.org/plenary/SESSION_4/Session%204_P2%20-%20SDMX%20Oriented%20Dissemination%20System%20Implementation%20Case%20Study%20at%20the%20Central%20Bank%20of%20Malta%20%28I.%20Cagan%29.pdf)).
- **CSV and Excel remain first-class formats even inside modern SDMX ecosystems:** The SDMX 3.0/3.1 technical specifications explicitly maintain **SDMX-CSV** alongside SDMX-ML and SDMX-JSON ([SDMX Official Technical Specifications](https://sdmx.org/?page_id=5008)), and the 2023 BIS IFC survey records active central bank usage of spreadsheet-bridging tools such as the **ILOSTAT Excel Add-in** alongside programmatic connectors ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).

#### 2025–2026 Modernization Trends
- **Structural shift from Excel/proprietary 4GLs to Python, R, and BI tools:** The 2023 BIS-IFC time-series survey explicitly found that **"Excel use is falling, with analysts increasingly using open source analytical tools such as R and Python, and specialist data visualisation tools such as Tableau,"** which is a primary catalyst pushing central banks away from closed legacy databases ([Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf); [BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)). On the NSO side, the Irish Central Statistics Office committed to replacing its extensive SAS libraries with R over a 5-year period to be fully operational by **end-2027** ([BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).
- **Open-source Python and R API clients for automated SDMX ingestion:** Central banks are increasingly automating ingestion via open-source libraries—specifically **`pandaSDMX`**, **`pysdmx`** (developed and open-sourced by the BIS via `sdmx.io`), **`gingado`** (BIS ML economics library), **`SDMXthon`**, **`rsdmx`**, and **`RJSDMX`**—as well as building R-based automated SDMX conversion pipelines (e.g., Central Bank of Malta) ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf); [Central Bank of Malta, SDMX 2025 Global Conference](https://www.sdmx2025.org/plenary/SESSION_4/Session%204_P2%20-%20SDMX%20Oriented%20Dissemination%20System%20Implementation%20Case%20Study%20at%20the%20Central%20Bank%20of%20Malta%20%28I.%20Cagan%29.pdf)).
- **Metadata-driven data lakes, lakehouses, and cloud adoption:**
  - **BIS MEDAL (Monetary and Economic Data Lake):** The BIS developed **MEDAL**, an entirely metadata-driven data lake built on **SDMX 3.0**, the open-source **Fusion Metadata Registry (FMR)**, and **`pysdmx`**, powering the BIS Data Portal (`data.bis.org`) with dynamic, metadata-generated publication tables that eliminate handcrafted spreadsheet tables ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025, Box C](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).
  - **ECB Big Data / Hadoop transition:** Beginning in 2019, the ECB launched a major project to replace its legacy macroeconomic statistics production platform with an open-source **Apache Hadoop** stack adapted to SDMX and big data workloads ([BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).
  - **Diverse storage modernization & cautious cloud adoption:** Across the 19-institution BIS-IFC sample, central banks exploring post-legacy architectures are adopting **SQL data warehouses, NoSQL databases, Apache HBASE, and bespoke in-house data lakehouses**, alongside growing consideration of **cloud services**—though BIS notes cloud migration carries significant cost, security, and strategic governance considerations ([BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf); [Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf)).
- **MLOps, automated anomaly detection, and Generative AI + SDMX (2024–2026):** Central banks are integrating **MLOps** into statistical production pipelines—including machine-learning outlier detection (e.g., variational autoencoders for multivariate time series presented at the August 2024 IFC Biennial Conference)—and building **LLM / NLP "universal data concierges"** grounded in SDMX metadata to simplify data discovery and prevent hallucinations, notably the **IMF's StatGPT** project and the joint **OECD–BIS SDMX+AI** initiative ([BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025, Box A](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf); [BIS IFC Bulletin No. 66, 2024/2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-bulletin-66-statistics-and-beyond-new-data-decision-making-central-banks)).

### Inferences
- A clear dual-track pattern persists: **institutional production pipelines** (scheduled headline macro feeds, official reporting) are steadily shifting to automated ETL, SDMX APIs, metadata registries (FMR), and cloud/lakehouse architectures, whereas **ad hoc economist workflows** (one-off tables, non-standard regional/sectoral cuts, rapid policy notes) still combine Excel workbooks with Python/R scripts and commercial add-ins.
- Even where central banks modernize their backend storage to lakehouses or SQL warehouses, retaining seamless Excel connectivity alongside Python/R APIs remains essential to prevent shadow-IT spreadsheets from proliferating during user transition.

### Gaps
- No global BIS, IMF, or OECD survey provides a direct quantitative estimate of the share of central bank economic analysis or ad hoc NSO series ingestion still conducted via manual Excel/CSV downloads, email attachments, or manual copy-paste.
- Verified 2025–2026 public-cloud vs. sovereign/on-premises deployment ratios specifically for macroeconomic statistical databases across central banks were not available from directly readable primary sources (an OMFIF cloud report returned HTTP 403).

## How do pre-release / embargoed access rules (e.g., IMF SDDS transparency standards) affect the technical delivery channel (secure transfer vs. public API at release time)?

### Takeaway
International transparency standards—led by the **IMF SDDS/SDDS Plus Integrity and Public Access dimensions**—require simultaneous, equal public release on pre-announced **Advance Release Calendars** and mandate full public disclosure of any internal government or central bank pre-release access. This legal and governance architecture directly splits technical delivery into two distinct channels: **public, automated APIs and NSDPs** that unlock only at the exact release timestamp for simultaneous public consumption, versus **strictly ring-fenced, exceptional bilateral secure delivery or physical lock-ups** (such as the UK ONS's documented 60-minute pre-MPC delivery to the Bank of England) when statutory monetary policy schedules require pre-release access.

### Cited Findings

#### IMF SDDS / SDDS Plus Governance Rules
- **Integrity dimension & mandatory pre-release disclosure:** Under both the IMF **SDDS** and **SDDS Plus**, the **Integrity** dimension requires adhering jurisdictions (covering NSOs, central banks, and finance ministries) to publicly identify and document on the **IMF Dissemination Standards Bulletin Board (DSBB)**—via Data Quality Assessment Framework (DQAF) metadata—whether *any* internal government officials outside the producing statistical agency have access to data prior to public release, specifying who receives it and under what conditions ([IMF DSBB SDDS Plus Overview](https://dsbb.imf.org/sdds-plus/overview); [IMF DSBB SDDS Overview](https://dsbb.imf.org/sdds/overview); [IMF DSBB DQAF Metadata Example — CPI](https://dsbb.imf.org/sdds/dqaf-base/country/MLT/category/CPS00)).
- **Simultaneous access & Advance Release Calendars (ARCs):** SDDS/SDDS Plus require **ready and equal public access** synchronized to publicly posted **Advance Release Calendars** and delivered via standardized **National Summary Data Pages (NSDPs)** supporting automated SDMX dissemination ([IMF DSBB SDDS Plus Overview](https://dsbb.imf.org/sdds-plus/overview); [BIS IFC Report No. 17, February 2025](https://www.bis.org/publications/ifc-report-17-sdmx-adoption-and-use-open-source-tools.pdf)).

#### Verified Institutional Case Studies of Pre-Release Restrictions & Exceptions (up to 2026)
- **United Kingdom — ONS abolition of routine pre-release (2017) and documented 2025–2026 Bank of England exceptions:**
  - Effective **1 July 2017**, the UK Office for National Statistics (ONS) ended routine pre-release access to ONS statistics for government and external users in all but **exceptional circumstances**, explicitly citing equality of access as a fundamental principle of statistical good practice; every exceptional grant must be approved by the National Statistician / Permanent Secretary and publicly logged on the ONS website ([ONS Pre-release Access Policy & Public Log, updated 22 October 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/prereleaseaccess)).
  - Despite the general ban, the ONS public log shows recurring, formally authorized **exceptional pre-release access granted to the Bank of England** across 2025–2026 specifically for market-sensitive macroeconomic series—including **Consumer Price Index (CPI) Statistics, Labour Market Statistics, and Pay As You Earn Real Time Information** (exchanges of letters published 15 September 2025, 11 December 2025, 13 March 2026, 10 June 2026, and 11 September 2026) ([ONS Pre-release Access Policy & Public Log, updated 22 October 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/aboutus/transparencyandgovernance/prereleaseaccess)).
  - In the **September 2026** exchange of letters between Bank of England Deputy Governor Clare Lombardelli and ONS's James Benford, the ONS agreed to **send the requested statistics exactly 60 minutes prior to the start of the Monetary Policy Committee's Indicative Meeting** on Monday 14 September 2026—demonstrating a direct, pushed, tightly timed bilateral delivery separate from public web/API publication ([ONS–Bank of England Exchange of Letters on Exceptional Pre-Release Access, 11 September 2026](https://www.ons.gov.uk/news/statementsandletters/exchangeoflettersbetweenthebankofenglandandtheofficefornationalstatisticsforexceptionalprereleaseaccesstoonspublicationsseptember2026)).
- **Europe — Controlled pre-validation and official transmission channels (Eurostat eDAMIS):** For European official statistics transmitted prior to public release, Eurostat operates **eDAMIS (EDAMIS 4)** with role-based visibility controls and an optional **Pre-Validation service** allowing data providers (NSOs and NCBs) to test structural and content compliance ahead of Official Transmissions without exposing pre-release payloads publicly ([Eurostat CROS EDAMIS Pre-validation User Guide, March 2026](https://cros.ec.europa.eu/book-page/edamis-pre-validation-user-guide)).
- **Australia — Inter-agency data-sharing governance (RBA & ABS):** The Reserve Bank of Australia has publicly emphasized the need for formal legislative and governance safeguards around inter-agency data sharing with the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) and government agencies to balance analytical access with confidentiality and market neutrality ([Reserve Bank of Australia Submission on Data Sharing and Release Legislation, August 2018](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/submissions/other/data-sharing-and-release-legislation/)). *(Note: Secondary search summaries also report ABS's use of physical embargo "lock-ups" for market-sensitive releases like CPI and National Accounts, and U.S. BEA/BLS embargoed lock-ups, though the direct ABS embargo URL returned HTTP 404 during verification—see Gaps.)*

### Inferences
- Embargo and equal-access rules create a **hard architectural boundary**: public NSO REST/SDMX APIs **cannot** expose pre-release figures to central banks prior to the Advance Release Calendar timestamp without violating SDDS equality-of-access principles or risking accidental public leakage.
- Consequently:
  1. **When a central bank has *no* pre-release access** (the post-2017 default for most UK releases and many SDDS-compliant regimes), its automated pipelines must poll or ingest from the NSO's **public API / website / commercial aggregator feed at or immediately after the exact public release timestamp**—making release-minute latency and API throttling critical operational concerns.
  2. **When a central bank *does* have exceptional embargoed pre-release access** (e.g., BoE MPC briefings 60 minutes prior to meetings), ingestion must occur via an **isolated, push-based, access-controlled bilateral channel** (restricted file drop, encrypted transfer, or physical lock-up) completely decoupled from public APIs and general internal economist databases until the embargo lifts.

### Gaps
- **Exact technical transport protocols for embargoed NSO-to-central-bank deliveries:** Public exchanges of letters (such as ONS–BoE) confirm *timing* (e.g., 60 minutes pre-meeting) and *scope* (CPI, Labour Market), and Eurostat documents eDAMIS for EU reporting, but NSOs and central banks generally do **not** publicly disclose the exact low-level technical transport used for sensitive pre-release drops (e.g., dedicated SFTP vs. encrypted email vs. closed web portal vs. authenticated embargoed API staging) for operational security reasons.
- Direct primary pages for ABS embargo lock-up rules (returned HTTP 404) and U.S. OMB Statistical Policy Directive No. 3 / BEA–Federal Reserve pre-release technical protocols were not directly fetched in this pass and should be verified directly if country-specific US/Australian deep dives are needed.

## What legacy statistical database platforms (like FIS FAME, SAS, EcoWin) are commonly used internally by central banks to store and serve ingested NSO series?

### Takeaway
**FIS FAME** has historically been the single most dominant internal time-series database across central banks—used in the past or present by roughly **three-quarters (14 of 19)** of institutions in the 2023 BIS-IFC time-series survey, including the Bank of England, ECB, and Banco de España—while **SAS** has been heavily entrenched at National Statistical Offices (Statistics Canada, Irish CSO, INSEE, Statistics South Africa) alongside econometric suites like **MATLAB, STATA, and EViews**. However, FAME is now in gradual structural decline (zero surveyed non-users are adopting it, and nearly half of current users are actively transitioning away—e.g., the Bank of England's ongoing **Future FAME** programme as of February 2026 and the ECB's post-2019 **Apache Hadoop** migration), with **no single replacement market product** having emerged.

### Cited Findings

#### FIS FAME Dominance, Strengths, and Transition Trajectory
- **Surveyed market penetration (2023 BIS-IFC survey of 19 central banks/statistical agencies):**
  - **12 of 19** institutions currently use **FAME** (Forecasting Analysis and Modeling Environment, released in 1981 and currently owned by **FIS Global** as part of its Market Data Suite / formerly MarketMap Analytical Platform) ([BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf); [Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf)).
  - Of those 12 current users, **5 are actively in the process of transitioning away** from FAME ([Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf)).
  - **2 additional central banks** previously used FAME and have **transitioned fully away**—meaning **14 of 19 (~74%, or "about three fourths")** have relied on FAME historically or currently ([Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf); [BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).
  - **0 institutions** not using FAME reported looking to adopt it; the **5 institutions** that never used FAME operate **bespoke systems** ([Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf)).
- **Verified institutional deployments and migrations:**
  - **Bank of England:** Uses FAME internally and is executing a multi-year modernization initiative—the **Future FAME programme**—which remained an active governance item reviewed by the Audit and Risk Committee (ARCo) and reported to the Court of Directors in **February 2026** ([Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf); [Bank of England Court of Directors Minutes, 12 February 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/minutes/2026/may/court-minutes-12-february-2026)).
  - **European Central Bank (ECB):** Used FAME for many years for the production and dissemination of macroeconomic time-series statistics before launching a major transition project in **2019** to replace its legacy platform with an open-source **Apache Hadoop** stack tailored to SDMX and big data ([BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).
  - **Banco de España (Central Bank of Spain):** Publicly documented using FAME across compilation, dissemination, and international transmission workflows ([BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).
- **Why central banks keep vs. leave FAME:**
  - *Retention drivers:* Unmatched speed and native functionality for traditional structured macro time series (including **FAME dynamic formulae** stored directly inside the database), operational stability, regulatory/security compliance, tight **Excel integration (FAME populator add-in)**, and very high migration costs due to deep analytical embedding ([Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf); [BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).
  - *Exit drivers:* Dwindling pool of internal FAME experts and IT support talent, **poor integration with R, Python, and Tableau**, inability to handle large/unstructured micro and big datasets, weak native searchability/metadata management, expensive commercial licenses, and closed proprietary documentation that hinders AI/LLM adoption ([Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf); [BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).

#### Other Legacy Platforms (SAS, Econometric Suites) & Post-Legacy Replacements
- **SAS (Statistical Analysis System) and econometric tools:** BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4 identifies **SAS** as a historically dominant proprietary platform particularly among **National Statistical Offices (NSOs)**—citing publicly reported deployments at **Statistics Canada, the Irish Central Statistics Office (CSO), INSEE (France), and Statistics South Africa**—while noting that central banks also rely extensively on commercial econometric packages including **MATLAB, STATA, and EViews** ([BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).
- **No single successor to FAME; rise of hybrid/decoupled architectures:** Across the 2023 BIS-IFC survey, institutions replacing FAME have diverged across **SQL data warehouses, NoSQL databases (1 respondent), Apache HBASE (1 respondent), and in-house data lakehouses (1 respondent)**. Because replicating FAME's speed and convenience for traditional time series is difficult and starting from scratch carries high cost/risk, several central banks are pursuing a **"middle-way" / decoupled architecture**: retaining FAME strictly as a backend time-series storage engine while stripping away its legacy 4GL analytical layer and layering **open-source Python/R tools, external metadata catalogs, and BI platforms (Tableau)** on top ([Ryland Thomas / Bank of England, BIS IFC Bulletin No. 64 Paper 20, October 2023](https://www.bis.org/2026-07/ifcb64_20.pdf); [BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4, December 2023](https://www.bis.org/publications/central-banks-use-time-series-products.pdf)).

### Inferences
- The absence of a dominant commercial replacement for FIS FAME explains why central banks are simultaneously investing in bespoke cloud/lakehouse platforms (like BIS MEDAL and BoE Future FAME) *and* maintaining commercial aggregator subscriptions (like Macrobond and Haver) as front-end economist workbenches.
- Decoupling storage from analytics—using FAME, `.Stat Core`, or SQL/Hadoop/lakehouses as the governed repository while serving economists via Python/R/Excel APIs—is emerging as the pragmatic transition pattern through 2025–2026.

### Gaps
- **EcoWin usage statistics:** Although EcoWin (historically acquired by Reuters/Thomson Reuters/LSEG) was a well-known legacy macroeconomic database/charting tool in central banks, the 2023 BIS-IFC time-series survey (`Guidance Note No. 4` and `IFC Bulletin 64 Paper 20`) did not report institutional usage statistics or migration figures specifically for EcoWin.
- **Federal Reserve Board internal FAME status:** While BIS IFC Guidance Note No. 4 references the Federal Reserve Board's November 2023 conference noting similar shifts away from traditional setups toward open-source tools, public primary documentation detailing the exact current (2025–2026) status of the Federal Reserve's internal FAME databases was not retrieved in this scope.
- **Sample size limitation:** The BIS-IFC time-series product survey covered 19 institutions (predominantly advanced-economy central banks and statistical agencies in the IFC network) and may overstate FAME prevalence relative to smaller emerging-market central banks.
